                                  ABSTRACT
   Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natu-
ral language understanding and generation. However, their reasoning processes often
rely on shallow pattern matching over unstructured textual inputs, which limits their
effectiveness in scenarios that demand external domain knowledge, fine-grained entity-
level understanding, or rapid adaptation to evolving tasks under minimal supervision.
These limitations become particularly pronounced in knowledge-intensive applications,
dynamic real-world environments, and downstream tasks where labeled data is scarce.
   This thesis proposes structural augmented reasoning, a unified paradigm that integrates
explicit structured representations, including knowledge graphs and dynamically con-
structed weighted graphs, into language model pipelines to enable more precise, context-
aware, and adaptive reasoning. The thesis develops this paradigm through two com-
pleted studies and one ongoing investigation.
   In the first completed work, KGLink, we present a hybrid framework that couples
knowledge graph information with pre-trained language models for knowledge-intensive
tabular understanding. KGLink explicitly addresses the type granularity mismatch and
                                           xi
missing context issues that hinder existing column type annotation methods, yielding sub-
stantial gains in both accuracy and robustness. In the second completed work, GORAG,
we propose a graph-based online retrieval-augmented generation framework for dynamic
few-shot social media text classification. GORAG constructs and continually maintains
an adaptive weighted graph that supports threshold-free, input-specific retrieval through
minimum-cost spanning trees, and has been published at WWW 2026.
   Building upon these foundations, the proposed work extends structural augmented
reasoning to general-purpose retrieval-augmented generation systems through two com-
plementary directions: (1) advanced graph-based retrieval that produces explicit reason-
ing chains and influence-aware subgraphs, and (2) path-aware semantic subgraph match-
ing coupled with graph-aware caching mechanisms for improved retrieval efficiency. The
expected contributions span both methodological advances and practical impact across
knowledge-intensive applications.

                              INTRODUCTION

1.1        Research Motivation

Large language models (LLMs) such as BERT [10], GPT-3 [4], and GPT-4 [26], all built
upon the Transformer architecture [36], have produced striking advances on a wide range
of natural language understanding (NLU) and natural language generation (NLG) bench-
marks. By pre-training on internet-scale corpora and fine-tuning or prompting on down-
stream data, these models acquire surprisingly broad linguistic competence and surface-
level world knowledge. Despite these successes, a growing body of evidence indicates
that the apparent “reasoning” performed by LLMs is often a form of shallow pattern
matching over distributional statistics rather than principled multi-step inference grounded
in explicit knowledge structures. This gap between surface fluency and true reasoning has
become increasingly visible as LLMs are deployed in knowledge-intensive applications
such as scientific question answering, biomedical decision support, financial analysis, and
content moderation, where the cost of plausible-sounding but factually incorrect outputs
is high.

   Three concrete limitations crystallize this gap. First, LLMs lack reliable access to exter-
nal domain knowledge. The parametric memory of an LLM is bounded by its training cor-
pus, is expensive to update, and frequently leads to hallucinations [13] when the model
is queried about long-tail entities, recent events, or specialized domains. Knowledge
graphs (KGs) such as Wikidata [38] and DBpedia [2] encode structured, curated knowl-
edge about millions of entities and relations, yet integrating this structured knowledge
with the unstructured representations learned by LLMs remains a fundamentally open
problem [27, 15]. Second, LLMs exhibit insufficient entity-level understanding: they tend to
reason at the level of tokens or phrases rather than entities, relations, and semantic types.
As a consequence, tasks that require precise grounding to a specific entity in a knowledge
base—such as column type annotation, entity linking, or table understanding—continue
                                              1
to challenge state-of-the-art LLMs even when their language modeling perplexity is low.
Third, LLMs adapt poorly to evolving tasks with minimal supervision. In real-world deploy-
ments, the label space is rarely fixed: new categories of misinformation, new product
taxonomies, and new types of scientific entities emerge continuously, and the per-class
supervision available is typically only a handful of examples. Standard fine-tuning is im-
practical at this scale, and few-shot in-context prompting [4] is sensitive to demonstration
selection and provides no principled mechanism for retrieving the right context.

   A unifying response to these three limitations is to equip language models with explicit
structured representations. Knowledge graphs provide a natural substrate for the first lim-
itation by exposing curated entities, types, and relations to the language model. Hetero-
geneous graphs constructed from textual corpora—linking entities, keywords, exemplars,
and labels—address the second limitation by giving the model a principled topology over
which to reason at the entity level. Dynamically constructed and re-weighted graphs that
adapt to each input address the third limitation by enabling the system to retrieve evi-
dence in a manner that is sensitive to the specific structure of the query and the current
label space. The overarching thesis of this proposal is that integrating such structured
representations into language model pipelines—rather than treating LLMs as monolithic
black boxes—yields substantially more precise, context-aware, and adaptive reasoning.

   The retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) paradigm [24, 13] has emerged as the dom-
inant practical strategy for grounding language models in external evidence. In its stan-
dard form, RAG retrieves text passages by dense vector similarity [22] and concatenates
the top-ranked passages with the input prompt. While effective for many open-domain
question answering settings, flat vector retrieval suffers from several well-documented
weaknesses: it treats each passage as an independent unit and therefore loses the rela-
tional structure that connects entities across passages; it is governed by a fixed similarity
threshold or top-k cutoff that is poorly suited to queries of varying difficulty; and it pro-
vides no mechanism to express multi-hop reasoning chains over evidence. Recent work
on graph-based RAG, including GraphRAG [11] and LightRAG [14], partially addresses
these issues by organizing retrieved evidence into entity-relation graphs, but the resulting
systems still rely on LLM-generated graph constructions that are prone to hallucination,
employ ad hoc retrieval policies that ignore graph structure, and lack principled algorith-

                                             2
mic foundations for selecting reasoning subgraphs.

   This proposal argues that the next generation of language model reasoning systems
should be built around structural augmented reasoning: a paradigm in which language mod-
els are systematically coupled with explicit graph structures—both pre-existing knowl-
edge graphs and dynamically constructed weighted graphs—through principled retrieval,
matching, and inference algorithms. Under this paradigm, the language model is no
longer the sole repository of knowledge or the sole arbiter of relevance; instead, struc-
tured representations and graph algorithms shoulder the burden of organizing evidence,
exposing reasoning paths, and adapting to evolving inputs, while the language model fo-
cuses on what it does best: interpreting natural language context and generating fluent,
semantically appropriate output. The remainder of this chapter formalizes the specific re-
search problems this proposal addresses, summarizes the contributions of the four works
that constitute the thesis, and outlines the organization of the document.


1.2     Problem Statement

The unifying motivation outlined in Section 1.1 translates into three concrete research
problems, each centered on a different combination of structured representation and lan-
guage model. The first two have been addressed by published work; the third is the
subject of proposed future research that builds directly on the first two.


1.2.1   Problem 1: Knowledge-Intensive Tabular Understanding

Tabular data underpins enterprise databases, web tables, and scientific datasets, and un-
derstanding the semantic type of each column—a task known as column type annota-
tion (CTA)—is foundational for table search, data integration, and downstream analytics.
Two broad families of methods have been pursued for CTA. Knowledge-graph-based meth-
ods match cell values against entities in a KG such as Wikidata or DBpedia and aggregate
the types of matched entities to predict a column type. These methods inherit the rich
semantic granularity of the underlying KG but suffer from two persistent issues. First, the
type granularity exposed by a KG is rarely a perfect match for the granularity required


                                             3
by a downstream task: a KG may predict the fine-grained type Q5 (human) for a col-
umn whose target label in a benchmark is the coarser type Person, or vice versa, leading
to systematic misalignment. Second, the scalability of KG-based matching deteriorates
rapidly when the value vocabulary is large, when entity surface forms are noisy, or when
the column contains entities that are missing from the KG.

   Deep-learning-based methods treat CTA as a text classification problem over column val-
ues, often serializing the column (and sometimes its surrounding table context) and feed-
ing it through a pre-trained language model [10]. These approaches are flexible with re-
spect to the target type vocabulary and handle noisy text gracefully, but they consume
only the information explicitly present in the table. When the table provides insufficient
context—for instance, a column of bare numeric codes whose meaning is documented
elsewhere—deep-learning-based CTA models silently fall back on shallow lexical heuris-
tics. Neither family of methods alone satisfies the requirements of practical CTA: KG
methods are precise where the KG covers the data but brittle where it does not, and deep-
learning methods are robust to noise but blind to external knowledge.

   The first research problem of this thesis is therefore to design a hybrid framework that
combines the broad coverage and semantic precision of KG-based matching with the con-
textual flexibility of pre-trained language models, while explicitly aligning type granu-
larity to the target task. The framework must (i) recover column-relevant evidence from
a large-scale KG efficiently, (ii) reconcile mismatches between KG type granularity and
target type vocabularies, and (iii) compensate for cases in which the table itself provides
insufficient signal. This problem is addressed by KGLink [40], summarized in Section 1.3
and detailed in later chapters.


1.2.2   Problem 2: Dynamic Few-Shot Text Classification

Many real-world text classification settings are characterized by an evolving label space
and a chronic scarcity of labeled data. Social media moderation is the canonical example:
the categories of hate speech, misinformation, scam content, and platform policy viola-
tions shift on time scales of weeks or months, and the labeling effort required to keep pace
with these shifts is prohibitive. Even when each new label is provided with only a handful
of seed examples, downstream classifiers must generalize from those examples to a stream
                                             4
of incoming user-generated content that is noisy, abbreviated, and full of platform-specific
idiom.

   Standard few-shot learning methods such as prototypical networks [32] assume a fixed
embedding space and a relatively static class structure, and therefore degrade when the
label set evolves over time. LLM-based few-shot classification via in-context learning is
more flexible but is sensitive to demonstration selection, sees only a small fixed window
of context, and lacks any principled mechanism for retrieving the most informative seed
examples. Graph-based RAG approaches [11, 14] could in principle address this gap by
indexing the seed examples as a graph and retrieving structurally relevant subgraphs at
inference time. However, current graph-based RAG systems exhibit three failure modes
that make them ill-suited to dynamic few-shot classification. First, they construct a uni-
form index that treats all entities and edges with equal weight, ignoring the fact that some
keywords are far more diagnostic of a target label than others. Second, their retrieval poli-
cies are non-adaptive: they apply the same top-k or threshold rule to every query, even
though some queries demand only a single highly relevant exemplar while others require
a constellation of weakly related cues. Third, the retrieved context is often incomplete along
the most informative reasoning path, because pruning is performed by similarity rather
than by structural connectedness through the graph.

   The second research problem of this thesis is therefore to design an adaptive graph-based
retrieval framework for dynamic few-shot text classification that (i) constructs a heteroge-
neous graph linking labels, keywords, and exemplars with edge weights that reflect the
discriminative power of each connection; (ii) supports online updates as new labels and
new examples arrive without re-indexing; and (iii) retrieves a query-specific reasoning
subgraph whose extent is determined by the structure of the graph itself rather than by
an arbitrary cutoff. This problem is addressed by GORAG [39], also summarized below.


1.2.3    Problem 3: General-Purpose Graph-Augmented RAG

The first two problems are tied to specific application domains—tabular understanding
and dynamic text classification—but the underlying methodological gap is far more gen-
eral. As graph-augmented RAG matures into a default architectural pattern for knowledge-
grounded language models, three structural weaknesses become increasingly visible across
                                              5
applications. First, the graphs themselves are typically constructed by prompting an LLM
to extract entities and relations from raw text, and LLM hallucination during graph construc-
tion introduces phantom nodes and spurious edges that propagate downstream. Second,
the reasoning chains exposed by current systems are incomplete: retrieval returns a set of
nodes or a single shortest path, but rarely a coherent subgraph that supports multi-hop in-
ference. Third, current systems perform inflexible graph matching by relying on either rigid
exact-subgraph isomorphism in the style of VF2 [7] or purely embedding-based similarity,
neither of which captures the semantic flexibility required when the user query and the
indexed graph are described in different vocabularies.

   The third research problem of this thesis, posed as proposed future work, is to design a
general-purpose graph-augmented RAG framework that (i) extracts entities and constructs the
underlying graph using lightweight statistical signals such as TF-IDF rather than relying
solely on LLM prompting, thereby reducing hallucination at the source; (ii) selects an
evidence subgraph by jointly optimizing relevance and connectivity through a minimum-
cost maximum-influence (MCMI) criterion, exposing explicit reasoning chains rather than
disconnected node sets; and (iii) supports semantic subgraph matching with graph-aware
caching, so that path-level reasoning over indexed graphs can be performed efficiently at
inference time.


1.3    Overview of Contributions

This thesis contributes four interconnected pieces of work that together instantiate the
structural augmented reasoning paradigm. The first two have been published or accepted;
the latter two are the subject of proposed research and are described in this proposal as
future contributions.


Contribution 1: KGLink—hybrid KG and PLM for column type annotation.              KGLink [40]
is a framework for column type annotation that couples a knowledge graph with a pre-
trained language model. KGLink performs entity matching against a KG to recover can-
didate types for each cell value, but rather than relying on the raw KG type as a label,
it constructs a granularity-aware mapping between KG types and the target type vocab-

                                             6
ulary, resolving the granularity mismatch problem that limits pure KG-based methods.
In parallel, KGLink encodes the column values together with auxiliary KG-derived con-
text using a pre-trained language model, allowing the system to compensate for tables
in which the explicit cell content is insufficient. The two streams of evidence are fused
through a learned aggregation mechanism that adapts to the per-column reliability of
each source. KGLink achieves substantial improvements over both pure KG-based and
pure deep-learning-based baselines on standard CTA benchmarks and is the first frame-
work, to the best of our knowledge, to systematically address granularity and missing
context in a single architecture.


Contribution 2: GORAG—graph-based online RAG for dynamic few-shot classifica-
tion.   GORAG [39] addresses the dynamic few-shot text classification problem by intro-
ducing an adaptive weighted graph that organizes labels, discriminative keywords, and
exemplar texts into a heterogeneous structure with information-theoretic edge weights.
GORAG supports online insertion of new labels and new exemplars without re-indexing.
At inference time, it retrieves a query-specific subgraph through a minimum-cost spanning-
tree formulation that connects the input text to the most informative labeled exemplars
without relying on a fixed similarity threshold or a fixed top-k cutoff. GORAG demon-
strates that adaptive, structure-aware retrieval significantly outperforms both flat dense
retrieval and prior graph-based RAG baselines on social media classification benchmarks,
particularly in settings where the label set evolves over time.


Contribution 3 (Proposed): Advanced graph-based RAG with influence-aware sub-
graph generation.     Building on the lessons learned from GORAG, the third proposed
contribution is a general-purpose graph-augmented RAG framework that targets the struc-
tural weaknesses of current graph-based RAG systems. The proposed framework con-
structs the underlying graph using a TF-IDF-based entity extraction pipeline, which re-
duces hallucination relative to fully LLM-driven extraction. For retrieval, the framework
formulates evidence selection as a minimum-cost maximum-influence (MCMI) subgraph
generation problem and develops a greedy algorithm with bounded approximation guar-
antees, drawing on prior work on personalized PageRank [25, 3] and influence propaga-
tion. The output of MCMI retrieval is a connected subgraph that exposes explicit, multi-
                                             7
hop reasoning chains to the downstream language model, in contrast to the disconnected
node sets returned by current systems.


Contribution 4 (Proposed): Semantic subgraph matching with graph-aware caching.
The fourth proposed contribution focuses on efficient deployment of graph-augmented
RAG at scale. Once the indexed graph and the per-query reasoning subgraph are avail-
able, an inherent bottleneck is the matching of query subgraphs against the indexed graph,
particularly when matching must be semantic rather than purely structural. The proposed
framework introduces path-aware semantic subgraph matching, which generalizes classical
exact subgraph matching algorithms [7] to allow path-level semantic equivalence be-
tween query and index, and pairs this matching with a graph-aware key–value cache that
exploits the structural overlap between successive queries to reuse intermediate retrieval
and inference computations. The two components together aim to make principled graph-
augmented reasoning practical at production-scale latency budgets.

   Taken together, the four contributions form a coherent research arc. KGLink estab-
lishes that explicit KG structure can be reconciled with pre-trained language models in
a single hybrid architecture for a knowledge-intensive task. GORAG shows that the
same structural philosophy, when extended to dynamically constructed weighted graphs
with adaptive retrieval, transfers to a fundamentally different setting (few-shot classifica-
tion with an evolving label space). The two proposed contributions then generalize the
lessons learned: first, by formulating principled graph algorithms for evidence selection in
general-purpose RAG, and second, by addressing the systems-level question of how such
graph-based reasoning can be matched and cached efficiently. Each contribution targets
a distinct piece of the structural augmented reasoning paradigm, and the four together
provide an end-to-end view of the design space.


1.4    Thesis Organization

The remainder of this proposal is organized as follows.

   Chapter 2 surveys the body of related work that situates this thesis. Section 2.1 re-
views knowledge graphs and their use in tabular and entity-centric data understanding,

                                             8
including a discussion of column type annotation methods. Section 2.2 surveys retrieval-
augmented generation, contrasting dense passage retrieval with the emerging family of
graph-based RAG approaches and identifying the structural limitations that motivate this
thesis. Section 2.3 reviews few-shot text classification, paying particular attention to dy-
namic label settings and social-media applications. Section 2.4 covers the graph algo-
rithms that underpin the proposed methods, including subgraph matching, personalized
PageRank, and minimum spanning tree variants. Section 2.5 synthesizes the survey into
an explicit gap analysis that motivates each of the four contributions.

   Chapter 3 presents KGLink, the hybrid knowledge-graph and pre-trained language-
model framework for column type annotation. The chapter formalizes the column type
annotation problem with attention to granularity mismatch, introduces the KGLink archi-
tecture in detail, and reports a comprehensive empirical evaluation against both KG-based
and deep-learning-based baselines on standard benchmarks.

   Chapter 4 presents GORAG, the graph-based online RAG framework for dynamic

2.1     Knowledge Graphs for Data Understanding

2.1.1   Knowledge Graphs and Their Applications

A knowledge graph (KG) is a structured representation of factual knowledge in which
real-world entities are modeled as nodes and the relations between entities are modeled
as labeled edges. Large open-domain KGs such as Wikidata [38] and DBpedia [2] have
grown to encompass tens of millions of entities and billions of triples, drawn from col-
laboratively curated and automatically extracted sources. Surveys of the field [15, 27]
document a remarkable expansion in the construction, completion, and consumption of
KGs over the last decade and a corresponding proliferation of downstream applications,
ranging from semantic search to question answering, recommendation, biomedical infor-
matics, and decision support.

   The strengths of KGs as a substrate for data understanding are well established. They
expose explicit, machine-interpretable types and relations, support multi-hop traversal,
and provide a stable identifier space that resists the vocabulary drift typical of natural
                                             10
language. These properties make KGs particularly attractive whenever a downstream
task must reason at the level of entities rather than tokens, or whenever the system must
justify its outputs through traceable evidence. Recent work [27] explicitly explores the
unification of large language models and KGs, motivated precisely by the observation that
the parametric memory of LLMs lacks both the precision and the auditability of explicit
graphs.

   Their weaknesses are equally well documented. Coverage is uneven: long-tail entities
and recent events are systematically under-represented. Type granularity is non-uniform
across domains, leading to schema mismatches when KG types must be aligned with task-
specific label vocabularies. Entity surface forms are often ambiguous, and entity resolu-
tion between unstructured text and KG entries is itself a non-trivial problem. These weak-
nesses make pure KG-based approaches brittle in practice and motivate hybrid methods
that combine KG structure with the flexibility of learned text representations.


2.1.2     Knowledge-Graph-Based Column and Table Annotation

Column type annotation (CTA) and the broader task of semantic table interpretation aim
to assign each column of a table a semantic type drawn from an external vocabulary, often
a KG ontology. Early approaches such as TableMiner [45] and SemTab-style systems [1]
match cell values to KG entities, aggregate the types of matched entities, and resolve the
column type by majority vote or probabilistic inference over a candidate set. These meth-
ods are precise when the KG covers the data well and when the granularity of KG types
aligns with the target vocabulary, but they fail in two predictable ways. First, when the cell
vocabulary contains entities not in the KG—abbreviations, compound names, or domain-
specific identifiers—matching collapses and the column is left untyped. Second, when
the KG type granularity is finer or coarser than the target labels, the aggregation step
produces systematically biased predictions: a column annotated as Q5 (human) in Wiki-
data may correspond to either a generic Person or a specific Athlete type in the target
schema, and the KG alone cannot disambiguate.

   A complementary thread of work explicitly grounds tabular cells through entity link-
ing before performing annotation. Such methods improve precision by leveraging con-
textual signals from neighboring cells and column headers, but inherit the coverage lim-
                                             11
itations of the underlying KG. Despite considerable algorithmic progress on candidate
generation and disambiguation, no purely KG-based method has been shown to bridge
the granularity gap in a principled way, motivating hybrid approaches that combine struc-
tured matching with learned representations.


2.1.3   Deep Learning Approaches to Table Understanding

A second family of CTA methods abandons explicit KGs and treats column annotation
as a text classification problem over column values. Models such as Sherlock [17] and
TURL [9] serialize columns—and sometimes their surrounding tables—and feed the re-
sulting sequence through a pre-trained language model [10]. These models are trained
to predict a column type from a fixed task-specific vocabulary, sidestepping granularity
mismatch by learning the mapping from data to labels directly. They are robust to noisy
text, surface-form variation, and out-of-KG entities, and they have set strong baselines on
benchmarks where the table itself contains sufficient signal.

   Their weakness is the converse of the KG-based approach: when the table provides
insufficient context, deep-learning-based CTA models silently fall back on shallow lexi-
cal heuristics. A column of bare numeric codes, a column of acronyms, or a sparse col-
umn of free-text snippets provides little for the language model to encode, and external
knowledge that would resolve the column unambiguously—present in a KG—is simply
not available to the model. Recent table-pretraining systems such as TaBERT [43] and
TUTA [41] introduce hierarchical table-aware encoders that improve over generic text en-
coders, but they do not address the missing-context problem in any fundamental way.


2.1.4   Limitations of Existing Approaches

The two families above expose complementary strengths and weaknesses. KG-based
methods bring broad coverage and semantic precision but are brittle when the KG is in-
complete or when type granularity is misaligned. Deep-learning-based methods bring
flexibility and robustness but are blind to external knowledge and degrade silently on ta-
bles that lack explicit context. A small number of recent methods attempt to combine the
two, but typically as loose feature concatenations rather than principled fusions, and very
                                            12
few address granularity reconciliation explicitly. The gap motivates the KGLink contri-
bution of this thesis: a hybrid framework in which KG-derived candidates and language-
model-encoded context are reconciled through a granularity-aware aggregation mecha-
nism that adapts to the per-column reliability of each evidence source.


2.2       Retrieval-Augmented Generation

2.2.1     The RAG Paradigm

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [24, 13] couples a parametric language model
with a non-parametric retriever over an external corpus. At inference time, the retriever
fetches a small set of relevant documents conditioned on the input query, the documents
are concatenated with the query, and the language model generates an output conditioned
on the concatenation. The motivation is twofold: external corpora can be updated cheaply
without retraining the language model, and the retriever supplies the model with evi-
dence that may be absent or unreliable in its parametric memory. RAG has become a de
facto standard for knowledge-intensive applications, and a growing literature explores
its many components, from retriever architectures to prompt formats to end-to-end fine-
tuning.

   The basic RAG architecture has several attractive properties. It scales naturally to large
external corpora, separates concerns between retrieval and generation, and provides at
least a partial solution to the hallucination problem that plagues pure parametric models.
At the same time, it inherits the limitations of the retriever it is built on, and the choice
of retriever turns out to be the dominant factor in end-to-end RAG quality. The two ar-
chitectural families that have received the most attention—dense passage retrieval and
graph-based retrieval—differ fundamentally in how they organize and access evidence.


2.2.2     Dense Passage Retrieval

Dense passage retrieval (DPR) [22] encodes both queries and passages into a shared dense
vector space using a pre-trained encoder, typically a dual-encoder architecture trained
with contrastive objectives on (query, relevant-passage) pairs. Retrieval reduces to nearest-
                                             13
neighbor search over the passage embeddings, with similarity computed by inner prod-
uct or cosine. DPR significantly outperforms classical sparse retrievers such as BM25 on
open-domain question answering benchmarks and has become the default starting point
for RAG pipelines.

   Despite its empirical success, DPR has structural limitations relevant to this thesis.
First, it treats each passage as an isolated unit; relations between passages—shared enti-
ties, citations, conceptual overlap—are not represented in the index, and retrieval cannot
exploit them. Second, the retrieval policy is fixed: a top-k cutoff (or, equivalently, a sim-
ilarity threshold) is applied uniformly to every query, regardless of whether the query
is narrow and well-supported by a single passage or broad and supported by a constel-
lation of weakly related cues. Third, multi-hop queries that require chaining evidence
across passages are handled poorly by DPR: the most relevant single passage is rarely the
one that bridges two facts, and iterative retrieval techniques add latency and complexity
without restoring the structural information that DPR fundamentally discards.


2.2.3   Graph-Based Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Graph-based RAG methods address these limitations by introducing an explicit graph
structure over the retrieval index. GraphRAG [11] prompts an LLM to extract entities
and relations from each chunk of the corpus, aggregates the extracted triples into a global
entity graph, and performs hierarchical community detection to summarize the graph at
multiple resolutions. At inference time, GraphRAG retrieves a set of community sum-
maries whose level of granularity is matched to the input query and feeds them to the
generator. LightRAG [14] pursues a similar high-level architecture but emphasizes effi-
ciency, replacing the heavy global community summarization step with a lighter local-
then-global retrieval procedure that scales to large corpora.

   These systems represent a substantial advance over flat dense retrieval. By exposing
explicit entities and relations, they enable the language model to reason over a structured
representation of the corpus, and by retrieving connected substructures rather than iso-
lated passages, they support multi-hop queries more naturally. Empirical evaluations on
knowledge-intensive QA benchmarks confirm that graph-based RAG outperforms strong
DPR baselines, particularly on queries that require synthesizing information from many
                                             14
documents.

   However, current graph-based RAG systems suffer from three weaknesses that mo-
tivate the proposed work in this thesis. First, the graph construction step is dominated
by LLM prompting, which is prone to hallucination: spurious entities and edges enter
the graph and propagate downstream as silent errors. Second, the retrieval policies are
largely heuristic—community summarization at a fixed resolution, top-k neighbor expan-
sion, or similarity-based filtering—and lack a principled formulation of what evidence
the language model actually needs. Third, the resulting retrieved context is often a dis-
connected set of nodes or a single shortest path, neither of which exposes the kind of
coherent, multi-hop reasoning chain that downstream tasks require. The MCMI subgraph
generation approach proposed in this thesis is designed precisely to fill this gap.


2.2.4   Comparison: Vector Retrieval versus Graph-Structured Retrieval

The contrast between vector retrieval and graph-based retrieval can be sharpened by con-
sidering three axes. First, on representation: dense retrieval represents each unit of evidence
as an isolated vector, while graph-based retrieval represents evidence as nodes embedded
in a graph with explicit relational structure. Second, on retrieval primitive: dense retrieval
supports nearest-neighbor lookup, while graph-based retrieval supports nearest-neighbor
lookup augmented by traversal, expansion, and subgraph extraction. Third, on adaptivity:
dense retrieval is governed by a fixed top-k or threshold, while graph-based retrieval can
in principle adapt the size and shape of the retrieved context to the query. In current prac-
tice, however, graph-based RAG systems often fail to exploit the third axis, applying fixed
traversal depths or fixed community resolutions in a manner that mirrors the inflexibil-
ity of dense retrieval. Closing this gap—designing genuinely adaptive, structure-aware
retrieval policies—is one of the central concerns of this thesis.


2.2.5   Current Limitations and Open Challenges

Beyond the specific weaknesses of current graph-based RAG systems, several broader
challenges remain open. The construction of the underlying graph is rarely auditable: a
downstream user of GraphRAG cannot easily inspect which spans of the corpus support a
                                              15
given edge, nor can they correct hallucinated edges without re-running the entire pipeline.
The matching of query subgraphs to the indexed graph is largely embedding-based, with
little role for explicit path structure. And the systems-level cost of graph-based RAG is
high: graph construction is expensive, and per-query traversal can rival or exceed the cost
of generation itself, especially when the index is large. The proposed semantic subgraph
matching with graph-aware caching directly targets the last of these challenges.


2.3     Few-Shot Text Classification

2.3.1    Traditional Text Classification Methods

Text classification has a long history that pre-dates deep learning, with classical meth-
ods relying on bag-of-words representations, TF-IDF features, and linear classifiers such
as logistic regression and support vector machines. The advent of word embeddings
and recurrent or convolutional neural networks pushed performance substantially higher
on standard benchmarks, and the introduction of pre-trained language models such as
BERT [10] and its successors set a new state of the art. Standard text classification, how-
ever, assumes a fixed label set with substantial labeled data per class, and the methods
developed in this regime translate poorly to settings where labels evolve and supervision
is scarce.


2.3.2    Few-Shot Learning Paradigms

Few-shot learning addresses the data-scarcity side of the problem. Prototypical networks [32]
compute a prototype embedding for each class as the mean of its support examples and
classify a query example by nearest-prototype assignment. Matching networks [37] gener-
alize this idea by attending over the support set, while meta-learning approaches such as
MAML [12] explicitly optimize for fast adaptation to new tasks. These methods perform
well on few-shot benchmarks with stable label spaces but rest on assumptions that are
violated in dynamic settings: the embedding space is trained on a fixed task distribution,
and the support set is assumed to be known and complete at the time the prototypes or
meta-parameters are estimated.

                                            16
2.3.3   LLM-Based Few-Shot Classification

The emergence of large language models has reshaped few-shot text classification. In-
context learning, popularized by GPT-3 [4], frames few-shot classification as a prompting
problem: the model is given a small number of labeled exemplars in its context window
and asked to predict the label of a query. This approach is impressively flexible—it re-
quires no parameter updates and supports arbitrary new label sets—but it is sensitive
to the choice of exemplars, the order in which they are presented, and the wording of the
prompt. Subsequent work has explored automated demonstration selection, calibration of
label biases, and structured prompting, but the underlying limitation persists: in-context
classification provides no principled mechanism for retrieving the exemplars that are most
informative for a particular query, and its accuracy degrades rapidly as the label set grows
or evolves.

   A complementary line of work fine-tunes LLMs on small per-class datasets using
parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA. While effective for static label sets, these meth-
ods are ill-suited to settings in which new labels arrive continuously, because each new
label triggers a fresh fine-tuning episode and complicates serving and versioning.


2.3.4   Dynamic Label Settings and Online Learning

A growing body of work explicitly targets the dynamic label setting, in which the set of
classes evolves over time. Online and continual learning methods aim to incorporate new
classes without catastrophic forgetting of previously seen classes. In the text classification
context, this is particularly challenging because the cost of relabeling is high and the new
classes are often defined only by a handful of seed examples. Few-shot continual learning
combines the two challenges and exposes the limitations of both static few-shot methods
and continual learning methods: prototypes drift, embeddings become stale, and the sys-
tem has no principled mechanism to retrieve previously labeled exemplars that remain
relevant under the new label structure.

   Graph-based approaches are a natural fit for this setting because they can incorporate
new labels and exemplars as new nodes without re-indexing the entire structure. The
GORAG contribution of this thesis is built on this observation, but unlike prior graph-
                                             17
based classification methods that rely on uniform indexing or non-adaptive retrieval,
GORAG introduces information-theoretic edge weights and a minimum-cost spanning-
tree retrieval procedure that adapts the retrieved evidence to the structure of each query.


2.3.5   Social Media Text Classification Applications

Social media classification provides a particularly stringent test bed for dynamic few-shot
methods. Hate speech detection, misinformation identification, and abuse detection on
platforms such as Twitter, Reddit, and Facebook face a perpetually evolving landscape
of slang, evasion strategies, and platform-specific idiom. Public benchmarks for these
tasks are quickly outdated, and the cost of producing high-quality labeled data at the pace
of platform evolution is prohibitive. Surveys of the area document the heavy reliance
of moderation pipelines on continual retraining, transfer from related tasks, and brittle
keyword-based filters. Methods that can incorporate a handful of new exemplars and
immediately retrieve them at inference time—without retraining—are especially valuable
in this domain. GORAG is evaluated against this backdrop and demonstrates substantial
gains over both static few-shot baselines and prior graph-based RAG approaches.


2.4     Graph Algorithms for Information Retrieval

2.4.1   Subgraph Matching

Subgraph matching is the problem of locating, within a target graph, all subgraphs iso-
morphic to a query graph. Exact matching algorithms such as VF2 [7] and its descendants
traverse the candidate space using carefully designed feasibility checks that prune un-
promising branches early. Modern variants such as ISMAGS [16] exploit symmetries to
accelerate matching on large graphs, while approximate matching methods relax the strict
isomorphism requirement to allow noisy or partial matches.

   In the context of information retrieval and RAG, exact subgraph matching is rarely
the right primitive. Queries and indexed graphs are described in different vocabularies,
edges may be paraphrased rather than identical, and the goal is typically to find seman-
tically equivalent substructures rather than literal copies. This motivates path-aware se-
                                            18
mantic subgraph matching, which generalizes the matching primitive to allow path-level
semantic equivalence between query and target while retaining the algorithmic backbone
of classical methods. The proposed semantic subgraph matching contribution of this the-
sis builds on this generalization.


2.4.2   Personalized PageRank and Influence Propagation

PageRank and its personalized variant assign a relevance score to each node in a graph,
with personalized PageRank biasing the random-walk distribution toward a query-specified
seed set. Efficient algorithms for personalized PageRank, including bidirectional estima-
tors [25] and Monte Carlo methods [3], have made personalized PageRank computation-
ally tractable on graphs with hundreds of millions of nodes. These algorithms underpin a
wide range of applications, from recommendation to community detection to graph-based
learning to rank.

   Personalized PageRank is also a natural retrieval primitive in graph-based RAG: given
a query mapped to a set of seed nodes, the personalized PageRank vector identifies the
most influential nodes in the indexed graph relative to those seeds. The MCMI subgraph
generation problem proposed in this thesis can be viewed as a structured generalization
of personalized PageRank, in which influence is jointly maximized with structural con-
nectivity under a cost budget, producing a coherent subgraph rather than a flat ranking.


2.4.3   Minimum Spanning Tree and Its Variants

Minimum spanning tree (MST) algorithms compute, for a connected weighted graph, a
spanning subgraph of minimum total edge weight. Classical algorithms by Kruskal and
Prim run in near-linear time, and Steiner-tree variants extend the formulation to require
connectivity only over a designated subset of terminals, at the cost of NP-hardness and the
introduction of approximation algorithms. MST and Steiner-tree formulations are widely
used in network design, clustering, and visualization.

   In information retrieval, MST formulations are valuable because they expose a notion
of adaptive evidence selection: rather than retrieving a fixed top-k neighborhood, an MST-
style retriever returns whatever subgraph is necessary to connect the query to the most
                                            19
informative anchors, with the tree size determined by the structure of the underlying
graph. GORAG leverages this property, retrieving a query-specific tree from a hetero-
geneous label-keyword-exemplar graph with edge weights that reflect the discriminative
power of each connection. The proposed advanced graph-based RAG framework gener-
alizes the MST idea further, replacing pure cost minimization with a joint MCMI criterion
that balances cost and influence under a budget constraint.


2.4.4   Graph-Based Reasoning and Inference

Beyond retrieval, graph algorithms also underlie a range of approaches to reasoning.
Graph neural networks aggregate information from node neighborhoods to support node
and edge prediction; logical reasoning over KGs has been formalized through path-ranking,
embedding-based reasoning, and rule mining; and explicit symbolic reasoners traverse
multi-hop paths in KGs to answer compositional queries. The interface between these
graph-based reasoners and modern language models is an active research frontier, with
recent surveys [27] cataloguing both the opportunities and the challenges. The proposed
work in this thesis sits at this interface: rather than replacing language model reasoning
with graph-based inference or vice versa, it equips language models with explicit graph-
derived evidence and explicit reasoning paths, allowing each component to play to its
strengths.

3.1    Introduction

Tabular data on the Web and in enterprise data lakes constitute one of the most ubiq-
uitous formats for structured information. A central prerequisite for downstream tasks
such as table search, table augmentation, data integration, and question answering is to
understand the semantics of each column, i.e., to identify what real-world concept the
column denotes. This task is commonly referred to as Column Type Annotation (CTA).
For instance, given a column whose cells are {“LeBron James”, “Stephen Curry”,
“Kevin Durant”}, a CTA system should assign a semantic label such as Basketball_Player
drawn from a target type vocabulary.

   Two complementary lines of research address CTA. The first line exploits large-scale
knowledge graphs (KGs) such as Wikidata [38] and DBpedia [2], by linking cell values
to KG entities and aggregating the types associated with those entities [15]. The second
line, driven by recent advances in pre-trained language models (PLMs) [10], treats CTA as
a multi-class classification problem on top of contextual representations of the table [17,
9, 33]. Although both directions have produced strong results on standard benchmarks,
neither is fully satisfactory in practice: KG-based methods struggle to align the granularity
of KG types with that of the dataset’s label set, while PLM-based methods are limited by
the information that is locally observable inside a single table.

   In this chapter, we present KGLink [40], a hybrid CTA framework that systemati-
cally combines knowledge graphs with pre-trained language models in order to over-
come the limitations of either family in isolation. KGLink addresses two long-standing
challenges that we explicitly identify and analyze: the type granularity problem and the
missing valuable context problem. The remainder of this chapter formalizes the problem
(Sec. 3.2), presents the framework (Sec. 3.3), discusses our solutions to the two challenges
                                             23
(Sec. 3.4 and Sec. 3.5), reports an extensive empirical evaluation (Sec. 3.6), and concludes
with a short summary (Sec. 3.7). KGLink constitutes the first work of this thesis and
connects naturally to the subsequent chapters that further investigate trustworthy graph-
based learning.


3.2     Problem Formulation

We begin by introducing the notation that will be used throughout this chapter and giving
a formal definition of the column type annotation task. We then illustrate, through a
concrete example, the two challenges that motivate the design of KGLink.


3.2.1    Notation and Task Definition

A relational table is denoted by T = (h, C), where h = ⟨h1 , h2 , . . . , hn ⟩ is the (possibly
empty) header row and C = ⟨c1 , c2 , . . . , cn ⟩ is the list of n columns. Each column cj con-
tains a sequence of cell values cj = ⟨v1,j , v2,j , . . . , vm,j ⟩ drawn from a (possibly heteroge-
neous) value space. The header hj associated with cj is treated as additional contextual
evidence rather than as a ground-truth label, since headers are frequently missing or noisy
in real tables.

   A type vocabulary is denoted by Y and consists of a finite set of semantic types, e.g.,
Person, Athlete, Basketball_Player, Country. The type vocabulary is typically derived from
the type hierarchy of a KG G = (E, T, R), where E is the entity set, T is the type set (a
directed acyclic graph induced by the subClassOf relation), and R encodes the relation-
ships and the entity–type assignment τ : E → 2T . For each entity e ∈ E, τ(e) returns the
set of types declared in G.

3.2.2   An Illustrative Example

Consider the simple table shown in Fig. 3.2.2, with two columns: c1 contains a list of
person names (LeBron James, Stephen Curry, Kevin Durant) and c2 contains nu-
meric values (2.06, 1.88, 2.11). We highlight two challenges that any practical CTA
system must confront on tables of this kind.


Challenge 1: Type Granularity.                        Linking the cells of c1 to a KG yields entities all of which
share multiple valid types, including Person, Athlete, and Basketball_Player. A KG-only
method that simply takes the most frequent or most specific type may not match the gran-
ularity expected by the downstream application: when the target vocabulary Y contains
both Athlete and Basketball_Player, only the latter is correct; when Y contains only Athlete,
the former should be returned. The granularity that is appropriate for a given dataset
cannot be inferred from the KG alone.

Figure 3.2: High-level architecture of KGLink. The KG linking module produces a can-

Challenge 2: Missing Valuable Context.       The numeric column c2 contains values that,

that the column appears next to a column of basketball players, is implicit and may not be
expressible through standard table serialization techniques. A pure PLM-based classifier,
lacking access to external world knowledge, often errs on exactly these columns.

   These two challenges, which we have observed empirically on standard CTA bench-
marks, motivate the hybrid design of KGLink described in the next section.


3.3     The KGLink Framework

3.3.1   Overview

KGLink is composed of three tightly coupled modules, illustrated in Fig. 3.2. Given an
input column cj together with its surrounding table T , the framework proceeds in three

steps. First, the Knowledge Graph Linking Module (Sec. 3.3.2) maps cell values to KG enti-

didate type distribution pKG (y | cj ). This distribution carries explicit external knowledge
about what the column can be. Second, the Pre-trained Language Model Encoding Module
(Sec. 3.3.3) encodes the column together with its header and neighboring columns through
a serialized table representation, producing a contextual distribution pPLM (y | cj , T ). This
distribution captures the discriminative signal that arises from the table’s local context.
Third, the Hybrid Reasoning Module (Sec. 3.3.4) combines the two distributions by attend-
ing over the KG type hierarchy, resolving granularity conflicts and supplying external
context to the PLM where the table alone is insufficient. The final prediction is given by

The KG linking module is responsible for converting the cells of cj into a set of typed

Two technical challenges arise: (i) cells are noisy strings (e.g., LeBron James Jr.) or

types at different granularity levels, so we need a principled way to combine them.

where the gate α(cj , T ) ∈ [0, 1] is produced by a small feed-forward network from features

support. When PLM confidence is high and KG coverage is low, α is close to one, and the
model behaves as a PLM-only classifier; when KG evidence is strong and PLM confidence
is low, α is close to zero, and the model leans toward the KG. The classifier in Eq. (3.1)
reduces to selecting the type with the highest fused score Φ(y) subject to the granularity
constraints discussed next.


3.4     Addressing Type Granularity

We now revisit the type granularity challenge introduced in Sec. 3.2.2 and explain how
KGLink resolves it through the joint use of the KG hierarchy and PLM evidence.
                                                   30
3.4.1   Why Granularity Is Hard for KG-only Methods

A purely KG-based annotator that selects the most popular or the most specific type per
column tends to be miscalibrated with respect to the dataset’s label set Y. Consider again
the column of basketball players in Fig. 3.2.2. Linking these cells to Wikidata yields entities
each of which is annotated with many types, including Q5 (Person), Q937857 (Athlete),
Q3665646 (Basketball_Player), and so on. If Y contains both Athlete and Basketball_Player,
the most specific KG type is the right answer. However, if Y contains only Athlete but
not Basketball_Player, then the most specific type is out-of-vocabulary and the KG-only
method must back off to an ancestor whose level depends on the dataset. The choice of
this level is dataset-dependent and not encoded in the KG itself.


3.4.2   KGLink’s Solution: Hierarchy-Constrained Selection

KGLink resolves the granularity issue by combining three sources of information when
selecting the predicted type for column cj . First, the candidate set is restricted to types
that are present in the target vocabulary Y. Second, among these candidates, the KG ag-
gregation in Eq. (3.4) provides the cumulative evidence based on entity types and their
ancestors. Third, the hierarchy-aware PLM evidence p̃PLM (y | cj , T ) provides a soft pref-
erence that depends on the actual labels seen during training.

   We formalize the granularity-constrained selection as follows. Let Y⋆cj = {y ∈ Y |
sKG (y | cj ) ⩾ τy } be the set of types that have non-trivial KG support, where τy is a small
threshold. KGLink solves

            ŷj = arg max

where y′ ⊏ y means that y′ is a strict descendant of y in HKG , and γ ∈ (0, 1] is a gran-
ularity preference factor. Intuitively, KGLink prefers a more specific type whenever the
descendant’s fused score is comparable to that of an ancestor; otherwise it falls back to
the ancestor. This criterion adapts to the dataset granularity because the PLM has been
trained on the dataset’s label distribution and tends to assign higher probability mass at
the level the dataset prefers.

                                               31
3.4.3    Theoretical Justification

The behavior of Eq. (3.10) can be motivated by the following observation.


Proposition 1 (Granularity Consistency) Assume that the PLM is well-calibrated on the train-
ing distribution of Y, and that the KG aggregation sKG is monotone with respect to the hierarchy,
i.e., sKG (y | cj ) ⩽ sKG (y′ | cj ) whenever y′ ∈ Anc(y). Then the prediction ŷj defined in Eq. (3.10)
converges, as the size of the training set grows, to the most specific type in Y that is consistent with
the empirical distribution of column cj .


   A sketch of the argument is as follows. The monotonicity of sKG ensures that ancestors
are never weaker than their descendants under the KG. Calibration of the PLM ensures
that p̃PLM concentrates around the granularity level that dominates the training set. The
constraint in Eq. (3.10) then prefers the deepest type whose fused score remains within a
factor of γ of the best ancestor, recovering the dataset-preferred granularity in the limit. A
more rigorous statement and proof are deferred to future work.


3.5     Addressing Missing Context

The second challenge identified in Sec. 3.2.2 is that of missing valuable context: situations
in which the table-internal information is insufficient to determine the column type and
external knowledge is required.


3.5.1    When Does Context Go Missing?

Through preliminary studies on standard CTA benchmarks, we identify three recurring
patterns of missing context that hurt the performance of pure DL-based annotators:


• Headerless or noisy headers. Many real tables either omit headers or use abbreviations
   and codes (e.g., Ht, NLOC) that the PLM has rarely seen during pre-training. Without
   the header, ambiguous string columns become much harder to disambiguate.



                                                  32
• Numeric columns. As discussed earlier, numeric cells alone do not provide enough
  textual signal for a PLM. The decisive evidence is statistical (the value distribution)
  rather than lexical, and it lies outside the table.

• Short tables. For tables with very few rows (m ⩽ 3), the PLM observes too few cells
  to estimate a column’s semantic type reliably. Each cell becomes disproportionately
  influential, amplifying lexical noise.


3.5.2   Knowledge Graphs as External Context

KGLink supplies the missing context through the KG. For string columns, even a single
cell with a high-confidence link injects an entire entity profile into the candidate distribu-
tion pKG , including types and attribute values that indirectly characterize the column. For
numeric columns, the distributional matching against KG attribute values offers a strong
signal that is independent of any cell text. The hybrid reasoning module then leans on the
KG via the gate α(cj , T ) in Eq. (3.9): when PLM entropy is high or the header is missing,
α shrinks toward zero, transferring the prediction responsibility to the KG.


3.5.3   Integration Mechanism

The integration mechanism that enables this transfer can be summarized as follows. We
first compute three quantities that characterize the available context: the PLM entropy

3.6     Experiments

We empirically evaluate KGLink to answer the following questions. (Q1) Does KGLink
outperform existing KG-only and DL-only methods on standard CTA benchmarks? (Q2)
Does KGLink effectively address the type granularity problem? (Q3) Does KGLink ben-
efit from external KG context when the table itself is uninformative? (Q4) What is the
contribution of each module of KGLink?


3.6.1   Experimental Setup

Datasets.   We follow the standard evaluation protocol of CTA and conduct experiments
on two widely used benchmarks. SemTab [21] is the dataset released by the SemTab chal-
lenge series, containing thousands of tables annotated against the DBpedia type hierarchy
at fine granularity. VizNet is a large-scale corpus of Web tables harvested from open data
portals and visualization galleries, providing a complementary evaluation setting that is
dominated by short tables without an explicit KG type hierarchy. The statistics of the two
datasets are summarized in Tab. 3.1. The type vocabulary of each dataset is constructed
from the labels that accompany the benchmark, and, for SemTab, intersected with the
DBpedia hierarchy.


Evaluation Metrics.   Following prior work, we report Accuracy and Weighted-F1 over all
annotated columns, which jointly capture overall correctness and class-imbalance-aware
performance. For SemTab, we additionally disaggregate accuracy on numeric and non-
numeric columns to assess performance on heterogeneous column types.


                                           34
Baselines.   We compare KGLink against three categories of baselines that cover the most
representative CTA approaches in the literature. KG-only and shallow methods. T2K
Match [29] links table values to DBpedia and aggregates types via weighted voting. HNN
is a hybrid neural network classifier that combines character- and word-level features
over column values. Sherlock [17] is a deep classifier that uses around 1,500 hand-crafted
column statistics. PLM-based methods. TURL [9] is a transformer-based table encoder
pre-trained on a large Web table corpus. Doduo [33] fine-tunes BERT on serialized ta-
bles for column type and column relation annotation. TaBERT jointly encodes natural-
language utterances and tables for table understanding. KG-augmented and contrastive
methods. ColNet [6] combines a CNN-based encoder with KG features. RECA aligns
column representations with related-column context for type annotation. Sudowoodo
adopts contrastive learning over column representations. We further include three ab-
lated variants of KGLink itself, KGLink w/o msk, KGLink w/o ct, and KGLink w/o fv,
which respectively disable the column masking objective, the contextual table serializa-
tion, and the KG-derived feature vectors; these variants serve as fine-grained ablations of
the full model.


Implementation Details.     We use the BERT-base-uncased model [10] and the larger De-
BERTa as PLM backbones and fine-tune each for 5 epochs with the AdamW optimizer
at a learning rate of 2 × 10−5 and a batch size of 32. For entity linking, we use the public
Wikidata and DBpedia dumps and a sparse retrieval index with character n-gram features
(n ∈ {2, 3}). The hyperparameters β, γ, ρh , K, τlink , and τy are selected on the validation
split via grid search. All experiments are run on a machine equipped with two NVIDIA
A100 GPUs (80 GB), and we report results averaged over 3 random seeds.

3.6.3   Ablation Study

Tab. 3.2 already includes three ablated configurations of KGLink: w/o msk disables the
masked column objective during PLM training, w/o ct removes the contextual table seri-
alization that surrounds the target column with neighboring columns, and w/o fv drops
the KG-derived feature vectors that materialize the candidate type distribution. All three
ablations consistently underperform the full KGLink (BERT) and KGLink (DeBERTa) con-
figurations on both SemTab and VizNet, with w/o msk causing the largest drop on Accu-
racy (1.10 points on SemTab and 1.03 points on VizNet against KGLink (DeBERTa)). This
ordering confirms that the masking objective and the contextual serialization are the most
influential design choices, while the KG-derived feature vectors provide an additional but
smaller boost.

   To further answer Q3, we additionally analyze KGLink’s behavior on columns that are
notoriously difficult for PLM-only annotators (numeric columns) and on columns where
lexical signals dominate (non-numeric columns). Tab. 3.3 reports the Accuracy of KGLink
and the strongest five baselines on these two slices of SemTab.

   KGLink achieves the best Accuracy on both numeric (97.04%) and non-numeric (90.92%)
columns. The gap is most pronounced on non-numeric columns against contrastive base-
lines such as RECA (61.54%) and Sudowoodo (67.72%), whose embedding-only represen-
tations cannot fully resolve fine-grained semantic types without external evidence. The
robustness of KGLink across both column types validates the design of the KG linking
module: it not only addresses the missing-context issue on numeric columns but also
sharpens predictions on non-numeric columns by anchoring them in KG entities.

3.6.4   Granularity Analysis

To answer Q2 directly, we partition the SemTab evaluation set into three subsets accord-
ing to the depth of the ground-truth type in DBpedia: shallow (d ⩽ 2), medium (3 ⩽
d ⩽ 4), and deep (d ⩾ 5). KGLink improves Accuracy over the best baseline by 1.8, 3.5,
and 4.7 points respectively on the three subsets. The trend is monotone: the deeper the
ground-truth type, the larger the gain from KGLink. This is consistent with Proposition 1:
KGLink’s hierarchy-constrained selection can recover fine-grained types that lie below the
granularity reachable by either KG-only or PLM-only methods.


3.6.5   Context Analysis

To answer Q3, we further isolate the columns for which the table context is uninformative,
namely numeric columns and string columns in headerless tables. Compared to Doduo,
the strongest PLM-only baseline, KGLink improves Accuracy by 8.3 points on numeric
columns and 6.1 points on headerless string columns. The improvement on columns with
rich textual context is comparatively modest (1.6 Accuracy points), which is in line with
the design intent: when the PLM already has enough information, the gate α correctly
defers to it. Together, the granularity and context analyses support the claim that KGLink
addresses both motivating challenges in a targeted way.


3.7     Summary

In this chapter, we presented KGLink, a hybrid framework for column type annotation
that combines knowledge graphs with pre-trained language models [40]. We identified
two long-standing challenges in CTA, namely the type granularity problem and the miss-
ing valuable context problem, and showed that neither pure KG-based nor pure DL-based
methods can address them satisfactorily. KGLink introduces a KG linking module that
handles both string and numeric columns, a PLM encoding module that captures table-
internal context, and a hybrid reasoning module that fuses the two through a hierarchy-
aware mechanism with an adaptive gate. Extensive experiments on SemTab and VizNet
demonstrate consistent improvements over KG-only, PLM-only, and contrastive baselines,

4.1    Introduction

The previous chapter (Chapter 3) introduced KGLink, which leverages structural infor-
mation from knowledge graphs to enhance the robustness and reliability of large lan-
guage models (LLMs) on tabular reasoning tasks. The central insight of KGLink—namely
that external structured knowledge can compensate for the limitations of parametric reasoning—
naturally extends to a much broader class of problems, including the ones investigated in
this chapter. In this chapter, we move from static, well-curated knowledge graphs to a sub-
stantially more challenging setting in which the underlying knowledge structure must be
built, updated, and queried online from a noisy and continuously evolving stream of natural
language data. Concretely, we study dynamic few-shot social media text classification, a prob-
lem that lies at the intersection of natural language understanding, information retrieval,
and online learning, and that is of central importance to a number of socially-impactful
“Web for Good” applications.

Motivation: Web for Good Applications. Social media platforms have become a primary
medium through which billions of users share information, opinions, and emotions. At
the same time, they have become a fertile ground for harmful behaviors such as the prop-
agation of hate speech [8], the spread of misinformation and disinformation [31], and the
expression of suicidal ideation that warrants timely intervention [19]. Effective text clas-
sifiers for these tasks are a cornerstone of trustworthy content moderation, public-health
monitoring, and platform safety. However, deploying such classifiers in real-world social-
media environments is challenging for three reasons: (i) the set of target labels evolves con-
tinuously—e.g., new categories of misinformation emerge alongside breaking news, and
new slurs or coded hate-speech patterns arise to circumvent moderation; (ii) labeled data is
extremely scarce, because annotating social-media content is expensive, ethically sensitive,
                                             40
and often requires domain experts; and (iii) the distribution of incoming texts shifts rapidly
due to viral events, new platforms, and adversarial behavior. We refer to this setting as
dynamic few-shot social media text classification.

Limitations of Existing Approaches. LLMs such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 [4, 26] have
demonstrated impressive zero-shot and few-shot capabilities and have therefore become a
default choice for text classification when annotation budgets are tight. Nevertheless, they
suffer from two well-documented weaknesses that are particularly pronounced in our
setting: hallucination (the model fabricates plausible but incorrect class assignments) [20]
and outdated parametric knowledge (the model has no awareness of newly minted labels or
freshly emergent slang). Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [24, 13, 22] has emerged
as a principled remedy: by augmenting the LLM with retrieved evidence from an exter-
nal corpus, RAG grounds the model in up-to-date and verifiable information. More re-
cent graph-based RAG variants—GraphRAG [11] and LightRAG [14]—further enrich the
retrieval index with a structural representation of the corpus, typically a graph of en-
tities and their relations. Empirically, graph-based RAG outperforms vanilla RAG on
knowledge-intensive tasks, in particular when the answer requires aggregating evidence
across multiple documents.

   Despite these advances, we identify three fundamental limitations that prevent exist-
ing graph-based RAG methods from being directly applicable to dynamic few-shot social-
media text classification: the Uniform Indexing Problem, the Non-Adaptive Retrieval Problem,
and the Incomplete Context Problem. We formalize and analyze these limitations in Sec. 4.3.

Our Approach: GORAG. To overcome these limitations, we propose GORAG (Graph-
based Online Retrieval Augmented Generation) [39], a unified framework that constructs
and maintains a weighted keyword–label graph in an online fashion and retrieves input-
specific context through a customized minimum-cost spanning tree (MCST) algorithm.
GORAG is built around three key ideas:


• Weighted graph construction. Unlike prior graph-RAG methods that treat all extracted
  entities and texts uniformly, GORAG attaches importance and confidence weights to each
  node and edge, capturing both how informative an extracted keyword is and how reli-
  able its extraction was.

                                                41
• Adaptive retrieval via minimum-cost spanning trees. Given an unseen input text,
  GORAG extracts query keywords and constructs an MCST over the relevant subgraph,
  yielding an input-specific context that does not require a globally tuned similarity thresh-
  old.

• Online graph maintenance. As newly labeled data arrives, GORAG incrementally up-
  dates its graph—adding new keyword and label nodes, re-weighting existing edges,
  and pruning deprecated labels—so that the retrieval index always reflects the latest la-
  bel semantics.


GORAG can be plugged on top of any LLM backbone, requires no fine-tuning, and oper-
ates under a strict few-shot annotation budget.

Contributions. The contributions of this chapter are summarized as follows.


1. We formalize the problem of dynamic few-shot social media text classification and iden-
  tify three fundamental limitations of existing graph-RAG approaches in this setting
  (Sec. 4.2–4.3).

2. We propose GORAG, a novel graph-based online RAG framework that combines a
  weighted keyword–label graph, a customized minimum-cost spanning tree retrieval
  algorithm, and an online graph maintenance procedure (Sec. 4.4).

3. We conduct extensive experiments on social-media datasets covering hate-speech, mis-
  information, and suicide-risk detection, demonstrating that GORAG consistently out-
  performs standard RAG, GraphRAG, LightRAG, fine-tuned baselines, and few-shot
  prompting (Sec. 4.5).

4. GORAG is integrated into the broader thesis theme of structural augmented reasoning:
  together with KGLink (Chapter 3), it shows that explicit structures—whether mined
  from large-scale knowledge graphs or constructed online from streaming text—are a
  powerful inductive bias for reliable LLM reasoning.

4.2.4   Limitations of Existing Formulations

A careful analysis reveals that the standard graph-RAG formulation above is inadequate
for dynamic few-shot text classification in three ways.

1. Uniform treatment of nodes and edges. The function Retrieve typically uses a similar-
   ity score that treats every node and edge equally, ignoring the fact that some keywords
                                               44
  are highly informative for classification (e.g., a coded hate-speech term) while others
  are generic (e.g., the word “today”).

2. Global similarity thresholds. Most graph-RAG methods rely on a global threshold θ
  such that only nodes with similarity above θ are returned. Such a threshold does not
  adapt to the specific query and is brittle across heterogeneous datasets.

3. Static or coarse online updates. Graph-RAG indices are typically rebuilt offline. They
  do not naturally support per-step incremental updates required to track Y(τ) .


The next section examines these limitations in detail and motivates the design of GORAG.


4.3       Analysis of Existing Graph-RAG Limitations

4.3.1      The Uniform Indexing Problem

Existing graph-RAG approaches [11, 14] construct their index by extracting entities and
relations from a corpus and storing them as nodes and edges of a graph G. Crucially, the
construction step assumes that all extracted entities are equally informative and that the
underlying extractor is equally reliable on every input. Formally, for each text t ∈ D(⩽τ) ,
an LLM-based extractor Ext(·) produces a set of (entity, relation) tuples, and these tuples
are added to G with unit weight (or with a uniform aggregation such as count-based co-
occurrence).

   This uniform treatment is problematic for two reasons. First, social-media texts vary
dramatically in their informativeness. A short tweet that explicitly references a known
harmful narrative is far more diagnostic than a long but generic post that contains many
filler words. Second, the LLM-based extractor is not equally confident on every input: it
may extract spurious entities from ambiguous sentences, while extracting reliable entities
from well-formed sentences.

Concrete example. Consider the following two posts annotated with the label hate_speech:

        ta : “[ETHNIC SLUR] go back to where you came from!”
        tb : “Today the weather was bad and I am annoyed.”

                                                   45
A naive extractor might extract [ETHNIC SLUR] from ta and “weather” from tb , and
a uniform graph would create edges of equal weight from both keywords to the label
hate_speech. Subsequent retrieval would treat “weather” as a strong indicator of hate
speech, which is clearly undesirable.

4.4      The GORAG Framework

4.4.1    Overview

GORAG is composed of four interacting modules: (i) a Weighted Graph Construction mod-
ule that turns each labeled example (t, y) ∈ D(⩽τ) into a set of weighted nodes and edges;
(ii) an Online Graph Maintenance module that incrementally updates the graph as new
labeled data arrives; (iii) an Adaptive Retrieval module that, given an unseen query tq , re-
turns an input-specific context via a customized minimum-cost spanning tree algorithm;
and (iv) a Context Assembly and LLM Prompting module that assembles the retrieved con-
text into an LLM prompt and obtains the final prediction. Algorithm 1 summarizes the
end-to-end pipeline.


4.4.2    Weighted Graph Construction

GORAG’s index is a weighted bipartite-style graph G(τ) = (V (τ) , E(τ) , w(τ) ) defined over
two node types:


• Keyword nodes VK : words or phrases extracted from labeled texts in D(⩽τ) .

• Label nodes VL : one node per active label y ∈ Y(τ) .


Edges E(τ) ⊆ VK × VL connect keywords to labels they are associated with. Each edge
e = (k, y) carries a weight w(τ) (k, y) ∈ R>0 that quantifies how strongly keyword k is

associated with label y, taking into account both the importance of k in identifying y and
the confidence of the underlying extraction.

Keyword extraction with confidence. For each labeled example (t, y), we prompt an LLM
extractor Ext(·) to produce a set of candidate keywords Kt = {k1 , k2 , . . . } together with a
per-keyword confidence score c(ki | t) ∈ [0, 1]. The confidence score is obtained either
via the LLM’s logit-based self-consistency or via prompting it to rate its own extraction.
Intuitively, c(ki | t) captures how reliable the extractor is when extracting ki from t.

Text-level importance. Different labeled texts have different diagnostic value. We define a
text-level importance score IT (t, y) that reflects how informative the text t is in identifying
label y. A simple realization is

sophisticated approximation algorithms do not significantly improve performance while
incurring substantial overhead.

4.5.4   Analysis

Quality of retrieved context. We perform a qualitative analysis on 50 randomly sampled

that GORAG’s MCST consistently surfaces multi-faceted evidence: e.g., for a query about
a coded conspiracy theory, the MCST connects an explicit narrative keyword to two se-

contrast, standard RAG returns only the densest set of neighbors, which often consists of
paraphrases of the query and provides little discriminative information.

at τ0 = T/2. GORAG maintains its accuracy on both pre-existing and newly introduced
labels, while the strongest baseline (LightRAG) suffers an immediate drop of ∼8 macro-F1
points on the new labels and only partially recovers over subsequent steps. This confirms
that GORAG’s online graph maintenance integrates new labels efficiently.

Sensitivity to hyperparameters. We sweep the neighborhood size M ∈ {5, 10, 20, 40, 80}
and the maximum tree size S ∈ {10, 20, 30, 50}. Performance is stable for M ⩾ 10 and
S ⩾ 20, with diminishing returns beyond M = 20 and S = 30. These observations are
consistent with the complexity analysis in Sec. 9 and confirm that the retrieval cost can be
controlled without sacrificing accuracy.

4.6    Summary

In this chapter, we introduced GORAG, a graph-based online retrieval-augmented gen-
eration framework for dynamic few-shot social-media text classification. We formalized
the problem (Sec. 4.2), identified three fundamental limitations of existing graph-RAG
methods—uniform indexing, non-adaptive retrieval, and incomplete context—and pro-
posed three innovations to address them (Sec. 4.4): (i) a weighted keyword–label graph
whose edge weights encode both importance and confidence; (ii) a customized minimum-
cost spanning tree retrieval algorithm that adapts to each input without a globally tuned
threshold; and (iii) an online maintenance procedure that lazily updates the graph as the
label set evolves. Extensive experiments on three socially-impactful classification tasks
(Sec. 4.5) demonstrate that GORAG consistently outperforms standard RAG, GraphRAG,
LightRAG, fine-tuned baselines, and few-shot prompting, with the largest gains in the
strict few-shot regime and on newly introduced labels.

Connection to the broader thesis theme. GORAG operationalizes the thesis principle
of structural augmented reasoning: it shows that a graph structure—when constructed with
carefully designed importance and confidence weights and queried via a problem-specific
                                             59
spanning-tree procedure—is a powerful inductive bias for grounding LLM predictions in
verifiable evidence. Together with KGLink (Chapter 3), which leverages static knowl-
edge graphs to enhance LLM tabular reasoning, GORAG completes a two-pronged study
of structural augmentation: KGLink demonstrates the value of external, statically curated
structures, while GORAG demonstrates the value of internal, dynamically constructed struc-
tures.

Future directions. The work opens several future directions, which we discuss in detail
in Chapter 5 (Future Work):


• Hybrid structures that combine GORAG’s online graph with external static knowledge
  graphs (à la KGLink) for queries that require both task-specific context and world knowl-
  edge.

• Theoretical analysis of the MCST retrieval procedure, including approximation guaran-
  tees for the underlying Steiner-tree-like problem.

• Cross-platform deployment, where the graph is shared (or federated) across multiple social-
  media platforms while respecting privacy and policy constraints.

• Robustness to adversarial behavior, where adversaries craft texts that exploit the keyword
  extraction step to inject misleading edges into the graph.

5.2.1   Motivation and Problem Definition

Retrieval-augmented generation [24, 13] has become the dominant paradigm for ground-
ing LLM outputs in external corpora. The standard pipeline encodes the user query with
a dense encoder, retrieves the top-k semantically similar passages, and conditions an LLM
such as GPT-4 [26] or GPT-3 [4] on the concatenated context. While effective for single-hop
factual queries, this design struggles when the answer requires composing evidence from
multiple documents or following relational chains between entities.

   Recent graph-based variants such as GraphRAG [11] and LightRAG [14] address this
limitation by first building a knowledge graph (KG) over the corpus and then traversing
or summarising the graph in response to a query. These methods have demonstrated
improvements on multi-hop and global summarisation benchmarks, but our analysis of
their design reveals three open problems that we plan to address.


Problem 1: Inaccurate graph construction.      Existing graph-RAG systems delegate entity
extraction to the same LLM that will later perform reasoning. When the LLM hallucinates a
non-existent entity, the spurious node is propagated downstream and contaminates every
subsequent retrieval. Because entity extraction is a fundamentally distributional task—
which surface forms recur as informative mentions?—we hypothesise that classical statistical
techniques can be more robust than open-ended generation.


Problem 2: Poor reasoning ability.     Graph-RAG methods cluster around two extremes.
Community-based methods (e.g. GraphRAG) summarise local neighbourhoods or detected
communities and therefore tend to miss long-range dependencies that span several com-
munities. Walk-based methods (e.g. random-walk or shortest-path retrieval) do reach dis-
tant nodes but return paths that are difficult for the LLM to convert into an explicit reason-
ing chain, especially when the retrieved structure contains cycles or branching evidence.
Neither extreme provides the LLM with a compact and explicit reasoning subgraph.


                                             63
Problem 3: Incomplete answers on generation-style tasks.           Empirical evidence shows
that on tasks requiring open-ended knowledge summarisation, existing graph-RAG sys-
tems can underperform plain vector retrieval, because they truncate the corpus to the
retrieved subgraph and lose the broader lexical context that BM25 or dense retrievers ex-
pose [30]. A truly competitive graph-RAG framework must therefore complement rather
than replace conventional retrievers.


Problem statement.      Let D = {d1 , . . . , dn } be a document corpus and let q be a natural-
language query. We plan to construct a knowledge graph G = (V, E, T ), where V is a set
of entity nodes, E ⊆ V × V is the set of relations, and T is a set of relation-triples (h, r, t)
with h, t ∈ V. Given q, the goal is to return an answer a that maximises faithfulness
with respect to D while supporting an explicit reasoning chain over G. We formalise the
retrieval step as the problem of selecting a subgraph Gq ⊆ G that jointly (i) maximises
a query-conditioned node-influence score and (ii) minimises a query-conditioned edge-
cost, subject to a budget constraint on |Gq |.


5.2.2   Proposed Approach

We propose a three-step framework. Step 1 constructs a high-precision KG from D; Step 2
retrieves an influence-aware reasoning subgraph for each query; Step 3 combines graph
retrieval with conventional sparse and dense retrieval to produce the final context fed to
the LLM. The pipeline is sketched in Figure 5.2.


Robust Knowledge Graph Construction

To mitigate Problem 1, we plan to decouple entity extraction from relation extraction. For
entity extraction, we will rely on a statistical TF–IDF pipeline rather than on the LLM.
Concretely, we will (i) tokenise each document and lemmatise candidate noun phrases; (ii)
compute the TF–IDF weight of every candidate against the global corpus; and (iii) retain
candidates whose weight exceeds a corpus-calibrated threshold, after applying linguistic
filters that remove stopwords, dates, and numerical literals. The resulting candidate set

5.3     Semantic Subgraph Matching and Graph-Aware Caching

5.3.1   Motivation and Problem Definition

The framework proposed in Section 5.2 exposes two data-management problems that are
general to any graph-augmented LLM system at scale.


Problem 1: Inflexible subgraph matching.     Classical subgraph isomorphism algorithms
such as VF2 [7] test whether a query graph Gq is topologically embedded in a data graph
Gd . They treat node and edge labels as opaque symbols. In a knowledge-graph setting,
however, two graphs that are not isomorphic may still encode the same information. For
instance, a query graph with a direct edge A → C ought to match a data graph that
mediates the same relation through an intermediate node, A → B → C, when the path
A → B → C is semantically equivalent to A → C. Strict topological matching misses such
equivalences and therefore degrades both recall in graph-RAG retrieval and precision in
graph-based data integration.
                                           68
Problem 2: Event-agnostic KV cache recomputation.            LLM inference relies on a key–
value (KV) cache to amortise self-attention computation across tokens. State-of-the-art
KV-cache eviction policies score tokens by recency or attention magnitude and are blind
to the structural role of the underlying content. When the prompt is constructed from
a retrieved subgraph, however, tokens that correspond to high-influence nodes carry far
more downstream signal than tokens drawn from peripheral evidence. An event-agnostic
eviction policy may discard precisely the tokens that anchor the reasoning chain, forc-
ing expensive recomputation when subsequent queries revisit the same subregion of the
graph.


Problem statement.      Let Gd = (Vd , Ed ) be a data knowledge graph and Gq = (Vq , Eq ) be
a query graph with node and edge attributes. The path-aware semantic subgraph matching
problem asks for the set of mappings ϕ : Vq ,→ Vd that are consistent with Gd either
topologically or via a path-level semantic equivalence relation ∼P defined over paths of
bounded length. The graph-aware KV cache problem asks for an eviction policy that, given
the set of subgraphs likely to be retrieved by an online query stream, retains in cache the
tokens whose exclusion would induce the highest expected recomputation cost.


5.3.2    Proposed Approach

Path-Aware Semantic Subgraph Matching

We plan to extend the VF2 enumeration backbone [7] with two modifications. First, we
will replace the discrete label-equality test by a semantic compatibility test that returns true
when the dense embeddings of the candidate label pair lie within a learned similarity
threshold. Second, and more importantly, we will introduce path substitution rules: when
the matcher fails to match an edge (u, v) ∈ Eq directly in Gd , it will attempt to match the
edge against any path u → w1 → · · · → wk → v in Gd whose linearised semantics is
judged equivalent to the query edge under a path-encoder model.


Path encoder.    We will train a path encoder Φ that maps a sequence of triples to a fixed-
dimensional vector. Equivalence between a query edge (u, r, v) and a data path π will

                                              69
be decided by the cosine similarity between Φ({(u, r, v)}) and Φ(π). We will mine train-
ing pairs from KG completion corpora and from the documents underlying the corpora
described in Section 5.2.3.


Candidate pruning.     Because admitting path substitutions enlarges the search space, we
will design pruning rules based on path length, edge cost (reusing the formulation of
Section 5.2.2), and upper bounds on the path-encoder similarity. We anticipate that these
rules will preserve the asymptotic behaviour of VF2 while exposing semantic matches that
are unreachable to purely topological matchers.


Graph-Aware KV Cache Mechanism

To address Problem 2 we will design a cache controller that scores each contiguous block
of cached tokens by an expected reuse benefit combining (i) the average node-influence score
(Equation (5.2)) of the underlying KG nodes; (ii) the empirical frequency with which the
corresponding nodes appear in recently retrieved subgraphs; and (iii) the attention mag-
nitude observed during previous generations. Eviction will prioritise blocks with low
expected reuse benefit. The controller will be agnostic to the underlying LLM and will be
implemented as a lightweight layer on top of standard transformer inference engines.

   We will additionally study a predictive prefetch variant in which the controller, after
observing a query that retrieves subgraph G∗q , proactively re-warms the KV cache for
high-influence neighbours of G∗q that are predicted to participate in follow-up queries
within the same session. The prefetch policy will be cast as a contextual bandit driven by
features of the query stream.

                CONCLUSION AND EXPECTED
                     CONTRIBUTIONS

   This chapter concludes the thesis proposal by summarizing the completed work, ar-
ticulating the expected contributions across theoretical, methodological, and practical di-
mensions, and discussing potential risks together with mitigation strategies. We also out-
line alternative plans to ensure the robustness of the overall research agenda.

6.1    Summary of Completed Work

The central thesis of this proposal is that explicit structured representations—such as
knowledge graphs and dynamically constructed weighted graphs—can substantially en-
hance the reasoning capabilities of language models when integrated into their processing
pipelines. The two completed works presented in this proposal provide strong empirical
evidence for this claim.

   In Chapter 3, we introduced KGLink, a hybrid framework that combines knowl-
edge graph representations with pre-trained language models for column type annota-
tion [40]. KGLink addresses two critical challenges that had limited prior approaches:
the type granularity problem, where fine-grained types are difficult to disambiguate from
coarse labels alone, and the missing context problem, where tabular data lacks sufficient
surrounding information for accurate type inference. By leveraging structured knowl-
edge from external knowledge graphs as an augmentation to the language model’s input,
KGLink demonstrates that explicit relational structure can resolve ambiguities that purely
text-based approaches cannot. The success of KGLink validates our core hypothesis in the
domain of structured data understanding.

   In Chapter 4, we presented GORAG, a graph-based online retrieval-augmented gen-
eration framework for dynamic few-shot text classification on social media [39]. GORAG
constructs adaptive weighted graphs that capture semantic and contextual relationships

                                            74
among candidate examples, and employs a minimum-cost spanning tree retrieval mech-
anism to select optimal demonstration sets for each input instance. Unlike prior ap-
proaches that rely on static retrieval strategies or fixed thresholds, GORAG adapts its
retrieval topology to the specific characteristics of each query. The publication of GORAG
at WWW 2026 confirms the practical value and novelty of graph-structural augmentation
for retrieval-based reasoning in dynamic, real-world settings.

   Together, these two works establish the feasibility and effectiveness of structural aug-
mented reasoning across distinct application domains—tabular data integration and so-
cial media content classification—laying a solid foundation for the more ambitious future
directions proposed in Chapter 5.


6.2     Expected Contributions

6.2.1   Theoretical Contributions

This thesis is expected to make several theoretical contributions to the understanding of
how structured representations interact with language model reasoning.

   First, we formalize the notion of structural augmented reasoning as a unified paradigm
that encompasses diverse instantiations, from knowledge graph augmentation (KGLink)
to dynamic graph construction (GORAG) to influence-aware subgraph generation (pro-
posed work). This formalization provides a conceptual framework for reasoning about
when and why structural augmentation improves language model performance, and iden-
tifies the key design dimensions—graph construction policy, retrieval mechanism, and
integration strategy—that determine system effectiveness.

   Second, the proposed work on MCMI (Minimum-Cost Maximum-Influence) subgraph
generation in Section 5.2 of Chapter 5 includes a theoretical analysis of the computational
complexity of the problem. We establish the NP-hardness of optimal subgraph selection
under joint cost and influence constraints and provide approximation guarantees for the
proposed greedy algorithm. This theoretical grounding ensures that our practical solu-
tions are anchored in rigorous computational foundations rather than purely empirical
observations.

                                            75
   Third, the path-aware semantic subgraph matching framework proposed in Section 5.3
of Chapter 5 contributes a formal framework for flexible query-document graph align-
ment. By defining path-level semantic similarity measures and establishing their relation-
ship to downstream reasoning quality, we provide theoretical tools for understanding the
granularity at which structural matching should operate.


6.2.2    Methodological Contributions

The methodological contributions of this thesis span both the completed and proposed
works.

   KGLink represents the first hybrid KG+PLM method that simultaneously addresses
both the type granularity and missing context problems for column type annotation. Its
multi-source integration strategy—combining entity-level knowledge graph information
with column-level statistical features—establishes a methodological template for augment-
ing language models with structured external knowledge in data integration tasks.

   GORAG introduces the first adaptive graph-based RAG framework with threshold-
free, input-specific retrieval for text classification. The key methodological innovation lies
in the online graph construction and minimum-cost spanning tree retrieval, which to-
gether enable the system to automatically calibrate the number and diversity of retrieved
examples without requiring task-specific hyperparameter tuning.

   The proposed influence-aware subgraph generation for advanced graph-based RAG
contributes a novel methodology for constructing retrieval subgraphs that explicitly ac-
count for the influence relationships between knowledge elements. The Personalized
PageRank-based scoring mechanism combined with MCMI optimization provides a prin-
cipled approach to selecting subgraphs that maximize reasoning support while minimiz-
ing hallucination-inducing noise.

   The proposed graph-aware KV cache integrates topological importance scores into
the key-value cache management of transformer models, representing a methodological
contribution at the intersection of graph algorithms and efficient inference. This approach
enables long-context processing that respects the structural relationships among cached
tokens, rather than relying solely on recency or attention-based eviction policies.
                                             76
6.2.3   Practical Contributions

Beyond theoretical and methodological advances, this thesis makes concrete practical con-
tributions to the research community and potential industrial applications.

   On the implementation front, the GORAG codebase has been released as open-source
software (available at github.com/Wyb0627/GORAG), enabling reproducibility and facil-
itating follow-up research. We intend to release implementations of the proposed methods
upon completion as well.

   The techniques developed in this thesis are directly applicable to real-world scenarios
of significant practical importance. KGLink addresses data integration challenges faced
by enterprises managing heterogeneous data sources. GORAG enables effective content
moderation and classification on social media platforms where label distributions evolve
rapidly. The proposed advanced RAG methods target knowledge-intensive question an-
swering, a capability increasingly demanded in enterprise AI systems, customer support,
and scientific research assistance.

   Furthermore, all proposed methods are designed with scalability as a first-class con-
cern. The greedy approximation algorithms for MCMI subgraph generation, the lightweight
scoring functions for graph-aware caching, and the TF-IDF-based graph construction ap-
proach are all intended to operate efficiently at production scale, making them viable can-
didates for deployment in real-world systems.


6.3     Potential Risks and Mitigation Strategies

6.3.1   Technical Risks

The proposed research involves several technical challenges that could potentially impede
progress.

   The MCMI subgraph generation problem is NP-hard, which means that exact optimal
solutions are computationally intractable for large knowledge graphs. We mitigate this
risk through a greedy approximation algorithm that exploits the submodularity properties
of influence functions to provide provable approximation bounds. Preliminary analysis

                                            77
suggests that the greedy approach achieves near-optimal solutions in practice while main-
taining linear-time complexity with respect to the number of candidate nodes. Should the
greedy approach prove insufficient for specific graph topologies, we have identified addi-
tional pruning strategies based on structural properties (e.g., connectivity, degree distri-
bution) that can further reduce the search space.

   Graph construction quality is a second technical concern, particularly given that entity
extraction from noisy or ambiguous text can introduce errors that propagate through the
graph structure. We address this risk by adopting a TF-IDF-based statistical approach
for initial graph construction rather than relying on potentially hallucination-prone LLM-
based extraction. This statistical grounding provides a more reliable foundation, and our
influence-scoring mechanism is designed to be robust to moderate levels of noise in the
underlying graph structure.

   The graph-aware KV cache mechanism introduces additional computation during in-
ference, which could potentially offset the efficiency gains from reduced context length.
We mitigate this risk by designing lightweight scoring functions that operate in constant
or logarithmic time per token, amortizing the graph-awareness overhead across multiple
decoding steps. Early design analysis indicates that the overhead can be kept below 5% of
total inference time while achieving meaningful improvements in long-context reasoning
quality.


6.3.2      Data and Evaluation Risks

Research in retrieval-augmented generation faces several data and evaluation challenges
that we have proactively considered.

   The availability of suitable evaluation datasets is a potential concern, particularly for
novel task formulations. We mitigate this by leveraging well-established public bench-
marks including HotpotQA, Natural Questions, MuSiQue, and 2WikiMultiHopQA for
knowledge-intensive question answering evaluation. These datasets provide diverse rea-
soning challenges and enable fair comparison with existing methods. For domain-specific
evaluation, we plan to construct targeted test sets from publicly available knowledge
bases.

                                            78
   The lack of unified evaluation standards for RAG systems presents a challenge for
demonstrating the superiority of proposed methods. We address this by adopting a multi-
dimensional evaluation protocol that assesses accuracy (correctness of final answers), faith-
fulness (groundedness in retrieved evidence), completeness (coverage of relevant infor-
mation), and efficiency (computational cost). This comprehensive evaluation framework
ensures that improvements are robust across multiple quality dimensions rather than op-
timizing for a single metric.

   For GORAG and its extensions to dynamic settings, simulating realistic label evolution
is inherently difficult. We mitigate this by using real-world temporal datasets from social
platforms where natural distribution shift is observable, rather than relying on synthetic
perturbations that may not reflect actual deployment conditions.


6.3.3   Timeline Risks

The proposed research agenda is ambitious, encompassing two substantial future projects
each targeting venue-quality publications. We acknowledge the timeline risk inherent in
this scope and have designed several mitigations.

   Each proposed project is structured to be independently publishable, meaning that
partial completion of the overall agenda still yields meaningful research contributions.
The advanced graph-based RAG project (Section 5.2) and the semantic subgraph matching
with graph-aware caching project (Section 5.3) can proceed in parallel to some degree, as
they address complementary aspects of the structural augmented reasoning paradigm.

   To further mitigate timeline risks, we plan to begin prototype development concur-
rently with theoretical analysis, rather than following a strictly sequential theory-then-
implementation approach. This parallel development strategy allows early identification
of practical challenges that might require theoretical reformulation, reducing the risk of
late-stage surprises.




                                            79
6.4        Alternative Plans

We have developed contingency plans for scenarios where primary approaches encounter
fundamental obstacles.

   If the MCMI optimization approach faces scalability issues that cannot be adequately
addressed through greedy approximation, we will simplify the retrieval structure to tree-
based representations with pruning heuristics. Tree-structured retrieval sacrifices some
expressiveness compared to general subgraph retrieval but offers guaranteed polynomial-
time complexity and has proven effective in related work on hierarchical document re-
trieval.

   If semantic subgraph matching proves computationally prohibitive for real-time ap-
plications, we will pivot to an approximate matching strategy based on graph embed-
ding similarity. By encoding subgraph structures into dense vector representations, we
can leverage efficient nearest-neighbor search infrastructure to achieve sub-linear retrieval
time at the cost of some matching precision.

   If the graph-aware KV cache demonstrates limited empirical gains over standard cache
management policies, we will redirect this line of investigation toward selective graph
summarization for context compression. Rather than modifying the cache mechanism
itself, this alternative approach would use graph structure to identify and compress re-
dundant information before it enters the context window, achieving similar goals through
a different technical path.

   Finally, if the primary proposed directions encounter fundamental theoretical or em-
pirical obstacles, we have identified cross-language and multilingual RAG as a viable fall-
back research direction. The structural augmented reasoning framework naturally ex-
tends to multilingual settings where knowledge graphs can serve as language-agnostic
bridges, and this direction aligns well with the expertise developed through the com-
pleted works.

6.5    Concluding Remarks

This thesis proposal presents a coherent research program organized around a unifying vi-
sion: that explicit structured representations serve as a critical bridge between raw knowl-
edge sources and the reasoning capabilities of language models. By making relational
structure explicit and algorithmically accessible, we enable language models to perform
more reliable, interpretable, and efficient reasoning across diverse tasks.

   The contributions of this thesis span a deliberate progression from specific applications
to general infrastructure. KGLink (Chapter 3) demonstrates structural augmentation for a
focused data integration task. GORAG (Chapter 4) generalizes the approach to dynamic
retrieval scenarios in social media understanding. The proposed future works (Chapter 5)
further extend the paradigm to general-purpose knowledge-intensive reasoning and effi-
cient long-context processing, addressing fundamental challenges in the rapidly evolving
landscape of large language model systems.

   We anticipate that the completed and proposed works will collectively advance both
academic understanding of structure-aware language model reasoning and the practical
capabilities of AI systems deployed in real-world applications. The structural augmented
reasoning paradigm offers a principled path toward language models that are not only
powerful but also grounded, efficient, and trustworthy—qualities that are essential for
the responsible deployment of AI technology in knowledge-critical domains.
