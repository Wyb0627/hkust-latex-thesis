                                      CHAPTER 6

  SEMANTIC GRAPH MATCHING FOR MULTIMODAL
                        REASONING WITH MGRAG

   This chapter is based on MGRAG: Semantic Subgraph Matching and Graph-Aware Caching
for Multimodal Retrieval-Augmented Generation, published in the Proceedings of the VLDB
Endowment, volume 19, number 9, pages 2480–2493, 2026 [1]. It studies how a query can
determine the evidence graph to construct, how paths can express semantic correspondences
across different graph topologies, and how the resulting structure can guide context computation.



6.1      Multimodal Evidence and Query Requirements
An answer to a multimodal query may depend on a relation that is distributed across text and
images. A document can name a person, an image can show an action, and another passage
can connect that person to an event. Retrieving each item by its similarity to the query supplies
candidate evidence, but the relation needed for the answer can remain implicit. A graph makes
the entities and their connections available as a retrieval object. Its usefulness depends on which
facts are extracted and on whether its representation can express the relation requested by the
query.
   This chapter considers a corpus 𝒟 = 𝒟txt ∪ 𝒟img and a query 𝑞 that can contain text, an
image, or both. A retrieval module selects evidence from the corpus, and a multimodal language
model generates an answer using that evidence. The research question is how to construct and
retrieve a query-relevant relational representation while controlling the cost of preparing and
processing the context. The answer-generation task remains grounded in the original documents
and images. The graph supplies an additional representation of their query-related content.
   Three decisions govern this process. The first concerns when to construct a graph. A corpus-
wide graph requires extraction before the query is known. Its entities and relations consequently
reflect the extraction prompt’s general description of the corpus. When an image contains several
people, objects, and actions, the details relevant to one query can differ from those relevant
to another. MGRAG places the query inside the extraction process and constructs the graph
incrementally from ranked evidence. The resulting graph is specific to the current information

   The third decision concerns the cost of processing retrieved evidence. Graph construction

multimodal answer quality and context-serving costs under the published MGRAG protocol.

stopping decision in the middle stage connects ranking, graph construction, and matching. Each

6.4     Query-aware Graph Construction

6.4.1    Representing the query

MGRAG asks the language model to extract entities and relations from the query. Some query
entities are known, such as the name of a person or an object appearing in the question. Others are
requested by the question. The extraction prompt represents those unknown components using
semantically meaningful placeholders, including types such as Person or Year. A placeholder
provides a textual representation that can be compared with evidence entities even though its
value has not yet been supplied.
   Figure 6.3 shows the query and evidence graph representations. The placeholder boxes de-
scribe missing knowledge in the query. Passage nodes connect extracted entities back to their
chunks. These connections let the graph carry both relational content and its association with the
original multimodal evidence. In a multimodal corpus, a passage node can represent an image
as well as a text chunk.
   The original example asks which year an Irandhir Santos film was released when its poster
shows people doing martial arts. The query graph connects the actor to an unknown film, the film

                                                        5
to a poster, and a person on the poster to an action. It also includes a release-year requirement.
The evidence graph contains named film candidates and a visual description of a man performing
a martial-arts kick in midair. The depicted film, The Assailant, is connected to the year 2009.
These facts are the content of the published illustrative example.
   The example separates identifying the film from reading its release year. The actor’s name
alone can lead to several film candidates, including the other film shown in the graph. The visual
relation supplies the condition that distinguishes the requested candidate. Once the candidate is
connected to the poster evidence, the release relation provides the requested value. The query
therefore needs a connected account of the actor, film, depicted action, and year. A list con-
taining a similar actor passage and an unrelated year passage would leave that correspondence
unresolved.
   The placeholders give the matcher explicit locations for these missing values. The film place-
holder can be compared with named film entities, while the year placeholder marks the relation
whose endpoint supplies the requested answer. Representing unknown values by their semantic
roles lets matching operate before those values are known. The path procedure then tests whether
the selected evidence entities are connected through semantically suitable relations. This is the
point at which the query’s information requirement becomes a constraint on the retrieved graph.
   The query graph expresses a retrieval requirement at entity and relation granularity. Its con-
struction is a model-mediated interpretation of the question. The downstream matcher operates
on this interpretation, including the wording chosen for placeholders and relations. A semantic
match therefore indicates agreement between the represented query and represented evidence.
Answer quality is evaluated separately using the original question and reference answer.

6.4.3    Matching as a stopping decision

After each graph update, MGRAG attempts path-based semantic subgraph matching against the
query graph. A valid match ends the construction loop. If the matcher returns an empty result,
the next ranked chunk is used to enrich the graph. The loop is bounded by the maximum number
of construction iterations 𝜇. Thus, the amount of graph construction depends on both the ranking
order and the matching outcome.
   This design uses semantic coverage as an operational stopping test. Coverage is evaluated
through the query graph’s entities and relations, so a match requires more than one highly similar
isolated entity. The procedure seeks a correspondence for the query’s relational requirements.
When the current graph lacks such a correspondence, additional evidence can introduce a miss-
ing endpoint, a relation, or a path between existing endpoints. The graph can therefore become
matchable through information accumulated across chunks.
   The iteration cap controls the amount of evidence examined. It also bounds the opportunity
to recover a fact that occurs late in the ranking. Reaching the cap and finding a valid match are
distinct termination events. The matcher returns an empty result when no valid correspondence
exists in its current graph. The cap, graph extraction, and matching threshold jointly determine
which context reaches the answer generator.

beddings yet differ in whether their connecting paths satisfy the requested relation. Conversely,
several paths between the same mapped endpoints can express different relations and receive
different semantic scores.
   The returned subgraph is assembled from the paths needed by the accepted query edges.
Paths can share intermediate entities and edges. Their union exposes those shared facts in the
evidence graph, while the query graph continues to specify which relational requirements were
validated. A graph assembled in this way need not have the same number of entities as the query
graph. Intermediate evidence nodes are precisely what permits a relation expressed compactly
in the question to be supported by a more detailed description in the corpus.
   This representation relaxes the requirement that the two graphs express a fact with the same
number of edges. In the family-relation example, the evidence path has two steps and the query
relation has one. Their correspondence is judged through semantic similarity. The procedure
retains endpoint requirements, so the selected relation must connect entities that themselves
correspond to the query. It combines entity-level alignment with path-level evidence instead of
treating either component as suﬀicient on its own.




6.5.2    Anchor selection and candidate mappings

MGRAG first computes pairwise cosine similarities between query entities and evidence enti-
ties. The highest-similarity pair supplies an anchor entity in the query graph and its candidate
counterpart in the evidence graph. If no valid match is obtained from that anchor, the method
tries other anchors. An anchor limits the subsequent search to mappings consistent with its
correspondence.
   Candidate mappings are enumerated using a similarity-based modification of VF2 [3]. VF2
supplies a graph-matching search procedure; the MGRAG adaptation uses the threshold 𝜃 in
semantic feasibility checks. Each candidate is then validated relation by relation through greedy
path search. A candidate entity correspondence can consequently be rejected because one of its
required relations lacks an acceptable evidence path.
   For each accepted mapping, the algorithm takes the union of the paths that support its query
edges. It averages the edge-wise semantic similarities to obtain a score 𝑠. The highest-scoring
accepted subgraph is returned. Dividing by the number of query edges does not change the
ranking of candidate mappings for the same query. It provides an average semantic score with a
common range across queries whose graph sizes differ.

The path search begins at the mapped source entity and maintains its current endpoint 𝑥. For each

Among extensions exceeding 𝜃, it selects the highest-scoring one, appends that edge to the path,
and updates the endpoint to 𝑦. This repeats until the path reaches the mapped target. The search

   The endpoint and threshold checks serve different roles. Reaching the target gives the ev-
idence path the required relational endpoints. Passing the semantic checks gives it a represen-
tation consistent with the requested relation. An attractive local extension can still prevent the
greedy search from reaching the target, so a failed search does not establish that all possible
paths fail. The algorithm returns the best result among the valid candidates it finds.
   All query edges must pass path validation before a mapping is accepted. This condition
prevents a single high-scoring relation from compensating for an unsupported required relation.
The selected subgraph may contain paths of different lengths, because the evidence required

by different query edges can have different decompositions. Construction then stops on that
accepted match; otherwise, a further ranked chunk is incorporated and matching is attempted

6.5.4    Published algorithmic properties

The publication states that path-based semantic subgraph matching is NP-hard. Its proof sketch
reduces subgraph isomorphism to a restricted instance with uniform embeddings and unit path
lengths. The result motivates the use of semantic path growth and constrained mapping enumer-
ation. The complexity of enumerating mappings remains relevant even when each individual
extension is selected greedily.
   The published Theorem 2 gives a threshold-based quality condition for a valid returned
match. Every matched query relation has a supporting path with cosine similarity at least 𝜃.
Under the strict acceptance test in Equation 6.9, the average score of an accepted mapping ex-
ceeds 𝜃 as well. This condition describes the semantic validation of returned evidence. It is
conditional on finding a valid match and does not require the algorithm to recover one whenever
such a subgraph exists.
   The technical report discusses the threshold in terms of a bottleneck transport cost [4]. With
a per-edge cost of one minus cosine similarity, the threshold limits the largest accepted local
cost. The explicit theorem statement in the publisher PDF is used here to specify the result. The
method’s empirical quality is assessed by the graph-matching substitutions and answer-quality
measurements in Section 6.9.


6.5.5    Serializing selected evidence

After matching, MGRAG serializes the selected triples into 𝑆𝑟 , separating facts with [SEP].
This sequence is combined with 𝑞 and the original chunks consumed during construction. The
serialization supplies an explicit list of entities and relationships to the answering model. Orig-
inal chunks remain available because extraction is a selective description of the evidence, and
some linguistic or visual details may lie outside the triples.
   The same entities and relations were involved in the earlier graph-construction calls. Their
tokens can therefore overlap with tokens in the answering context. MGRAG uses this relation-
ship to reuse cached computation from construction while updating states important to the new
answering context. The graph produced during retrieval thus becomes an input to the context-
computation policy as well as to the language model’s prompt.

6.6        Graph-aware KV Recomputation

6.6.1       Reuse and selective updates

A transformer caches key and value projections so that earlier token states can be reused. In
MGRAG, content processed during graph construction appears again in the query-answering
context. Recomputing every state repeats part of this work. Reusing every state can retain
representations calculated under a different surrounding sequence. The cache policy chooses a
subset for recomputation and reuses the others.
   Let 𝜙(𝑡) be the recomputation score of token 𝑡. At each transformer layer, MGRAG re-
computes the tokens in the highest 𝑏% of scores and reuses the cached states for the remaining
tokens. The published description uses a fixed-size cache with least-recently-used eviction. Its
proposed policy concerns the ranking of token states for selective recomputation. The budget is
represented by 𝑏; the numerical experiment tables do not specify a fixed value for that budget.
   Figure 6.4 shows how the graph contributes. Query-related events are represented by entities
and relations. The score combines attention to the query, attention to the graph sequence, and a
feature derived from the topology of the associated fact. The goal of the added term is to give
tokens access to an event-level structural signal alongside token-level attention.

The token-to-fact association follows the entity and relation token spans in the serialized repre-

method does not train a separate token-importance predictor on the evaluation queries. Its score
instead uses the graph and attention information available during processing.

   Four metrics characterize answers. Semantic similarity (SIM.) is the cosine similarity be-
tween bge-base-en-v1.5 representations of generated and reference answers. Accuracy (ACC.)
is the reported proportion of correct answers. ROUGE-L (ROG.) evaluates longest-common-
subsequence overlap [13], and BLEU-1 evaluates modified unigram precision [14]. The paper

including the label BLEU used for BLEU-1.
   The four answer metrics reflect different properties. Embedding similarity compares seman-
tic proximity at the answer level. ROUGE-L and BLEU-1 retain sensitivity to the wording shared
with the reference. Accuracy provides the task’s correctness measurement. They are shown to-
gether because agreement in one measure does not imply identical behavior in the others. The
publication reports point estimates for these experiments.
   The complete-system baselines use different retrieval units. VanillaRAG returns dense-
retrieved chunks, and R1-Searcher incorporates reinforcement learning into search and ques-
tion answering. GraphRAG organizes its graph into communities and prepares summaries;
LightRAG retrieves graph elements and neighborhoods through its selected search mode. Hip-

                                                  15
poRAG2 adds passage nodes and uses Personalized PageRank for retrieval. In the published
experiment, these systems are compared as implemented pipelines. Their results include their
own indexing and evidence-selection choices, while the shared backbone and evaluation metrics
provide the common answering conditions.
   The construction and matching substitutions supply a second level of comparison. Their role
is to replace one part of MGRAG while retaining the surrounding question-answering task. For
example, the graph-construction alternatives change which facts are available before matching.
A matching alternative changes how the existing representation is aligned with the query. This
separation is useful when an answer contains information from several source items: a missing
fact and an unsuitable correspondence can both lower answer quality, but they concern different
stages of the pipeline.
   The comparisons have four roles. Complete-system baselines measure the combined re-
trieval and generation pipeline. Graph-construction substitutions replace the evidence extraction
component. Graph-matching substitutions replace the correspondence mechanism. Cache com-
parisons and inverse ablations vary context-serving components. Keeping these roles distinct
makes it possible to relate each experiment to the particular decision it tests.

                  The threshold changes which entity correspondences and path extensions can pass valida-
  tion. A higher value demands closer embedding agreement, while a lower value permits more
  candidates. The path-length cap determines how many relations may be used to express a query

relation. Its effect therefore concerns both representational flexibility and search. These roles
explain why the two parameters belong to the matching mechanism, while their empirical choice
remains tied to the reported evaluation.



6.12      Discussion and Chapter Summary
MGRAG gives structure three connected responsibilities. Query-aware extraction determines
which facts enter the graph. Path-based matching determines whether those facts express the
relational requirements of the query. Graph features then influence the selection of token states
for recomputation. The graph is consequently involved in constructing, selecting, and processing
evidence, rather than serving only as a store from which a fixed number of items is retrieved.
   The experiments support this design at several levels. Complete-system results report the
highest values across the seven datasets’ four answer metrics. Construction and matching substi-
tutions evaluate the evidence representation and correspondence choices. The hop-group com-
parison shows additional quality gains for questions requiring more relational steps, together
with added time. Cache comparisons and inverse ablations retain the context-serving contribu-
tion and expose its dependence on the surrounding retrieval pipeline.
   Several conditions delimit the interpretation. Captions influence the coarse order of image
evidence, and model-generated extraction determines which facts are available for matching. A
valid semantic match is assessed against the extracted query graph and an embedding thresh-
old; answer correctness is evaluated downstream. The maximum construction and path budgets
restrict the search. The published runtime measurements use one hardware and model configura-
tion, and the tables do not provide the fixed numerical recomputation budget needed to attribute
a cost to a particular 𝑏 value. These conditions identify the representation and computation
choices on which the reported results depend.
   For the thesis’s central question, the chapter establishes a concrete extension from connected
textual evidence to query-dependent multimodal evidence. Semantic paths allow relations to
be represented at different granularities across the query and evidence graphs. Selective KV
recomputation shows a further use of the retrieved structure in context processing. The following
discussion compares these roles with the representations and retrieval objects studied in KGLink,
GORAG, and AGRAG, using their respective tasks and published evidence.
